01
Grounded vs Ungrounded
Not all AI answers are created equal — some search, some guess
[TIMING: 0.5 min] [TRANSITION: "Let's start with the most important distinction in AI research."] Key talking points: • This chapter introduces the fundamental concept: grounded vs ungrounded AI • Most people don't realise there are fundamentally different TYPES of AI answers • Some tools search real sources and cite them; others generate from patterns in training data • Understanding this difference is the single most important research skill with AI

The Problem with General Chat

⚠️ What Goes Wrong
• AI generates plausible-sounding answers without sources
• Sounds confident even when completely wrong
• Fine for brainstorming, dangerous for research
• No way to verify without doing the research yourself
🧪 Real Examples of Confident Errors
• "The UK GDPR fine limit is £500,000" (outdated — it's £17.5M)
• "This paper by Smith et al. (2022)..." (paper doesn't exist)
• "The regulation was updated in March 2024" (actually June 2023)
• "According to the ONS report..." (fabricated citation)
🎯 The problem isn't that AI is always wrong — it's that you can't tell WHEN it's wrong without checking
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "Here's why using general AI chat for research is risky."] Key talking points: • General chat AI (ChatGPT, Claude) generates text from training patterns — it's not searching the web • The problem: it sounds EQUALLY confident whether right or wrong. There's no "uncertainty signal" • Real-world consequence: professionals cite AI-generated "facts" in reports, proposals, emails — and get caught • This isn't rare — studies show AI hallucinates facts in 15-30% of factual responses • The examples on right are all things AI has confidently stated — all wrong Discussion prompt: "Which of these errors would cause the most damage in YOUR workplace?"

What "Grounded" Means

❌ Ungrounded (ChatGPT / Claude)
• Generates from training dataTraining data is the text AI learned from during development. It's a frozen snapshot — like an encyclopedia that stopped printing months or years ago.
• No real-time web access
• No citations provided
• May hallucinate confidently
• Best for: brainstorming, drafting, creative work
VS
✅ Grounded (Perplexity / Google AI Overview)
• Searches the real-time webGrounded tools actually perform web searches, read the pages, and synthesise answers from what they find — like a research assistant who Googles for you and summarises.
• Accesses current information
• Provides citations with links
• Claims are traceable to sources
• Best for: research, fact-checking, current events
💡 For research tasks: always start with a grounded tool. Use ungrounded tools for drafting your findings.
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "So what's the alternative? Grounded AI tools."] Key talking points: • KEY distinction: ungrounded AI generates from patterns; grounded AI SEARCHES and cites • Ungrounded isn't "bad" — it's great for drafting, brainstorming, rewriting. Just not for research • Grounded tools actually search the web, read pages, and cite what they found • The citation is the key feature: you can CHECK the source yourself • Neither is perfect — grounded tools can still misinterpret sources — but they give you a trail to follow • Name real tools: Perplexity AI, Google AI Overviews, Microsoft Copilot with web search enabled Discussion prompt: "Who here has used Perplexity or a similar tool? What was different about the experience?"

02
The Manager-Ready Brief
Structure your research so others can trust it — and verify it
[TIMING: 0.5 min] [TRANSITION: "Now that you know HOW to get grounded answers, let's structure them."] Key talking points: • Knowing how to find information is step one — presenting it well is step two • A research brief isn't just a summary — it's a structured, verifiable document • The format we'll learn is designed for busy managers who need to trust your findings fast • Key difference from regular summaries: a research brief always acknowledges what you DON'T know

Anatomy of a Research Brief

1. QUESTION
What specific question are we answering?

Why: Forces clarity. Vague questions = vague research.
→
2. SOURCES
What sources support the answer? (With links)

Why: Makes claims verifiable. No source = no credibility.
→
3. RECOMMENDATION
What do the sources suggest we should do?

Why: Research without action is academic. Managers need decisions.
→
4. UNKNOWNS
What couldn't we confirm? What gaps remain?

Why: Honest uncertainty builds trust. Hidden gaps destroy it.
🎯 The "Unknowns" section is what separates professional research from AI copy-paste
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "Here's the structure that makes research trustworthy."] Key talking points: • This 4-part structure works for any research task — from "should we switch vendors?" to "what are the new regulations?" • QUESTION: Be specific. "What are the GDPR implications of our new CRM?" not "Tell me about GDPR" • SOURCES: Every factual claim needs a traceable source. No source = opinion, not research • RECOMMENDATION: Connect findings to action. What should the reader DO with this information? • UNKNOWNS: This is the game-changer. Acknowledging what you DON'T know builds more trust than pretending you know everything • AI never volunteers its unknowns — YOU must add this section yourself Discussion prompt: "In your experience, do research reports usually include what they DON'T know?"
🔨 PRACTICAL

Write a Research Brief

  1. 1Choose a real work question you need answered (e.g., "What are the current rules on…?" or "What options exist for…?")
  2. 2Use a grounded AI tool (Perplexity, Google AI) to research the question — collect cited answers
  3. 3Structure your findings into the 4-part brief: Question → Sources → Recommendation → Unknowns
  4. 4Your "Unknowns" section must include at least 2 things you couldn't confirm or that need further investigation
⏱️ 10 minutes — use a real question from your work if possible | You'll swap briefs with a partner next
[TIMING: 10 min] [TRANSITION: "Time to build your first research brief."] Key talking points: • This is the core practical of the module — give them the full 10 minutes • Encourage REAL questions from their actual work — it's more engaging and useful • If they can't think of a question, suggest: "What are the latest guidelines on [something in your industry]?" • Walk around and check: Are they using a grounded tool? Are they noting sources? • The "Unknowns" section is the hardest part — many will want to skip it. Push them to include it • Remind: this brief will be verified by a partner, so don't fake the sources! Facilitation tip: Project the 4-part structure on screen while they work. Set a 5-minute halfway reminder.

03
Compare Before You Trust
When two tools disagree, that's your signal to verify
[TIMING: 0.5 min] [TRANSITION: "Chapter 3 — the comparison method."] Key talking points: • This chapter teaches a simple but powerful technique: ask the SAME question to two different AI tools • Where they agree → likely correct (but still worth checking) • Where they disagree → DEFINITELY needs verification • Divergence is a signal, not a failure — it tells you where to focus your checking effort

The Comparison Method

STEP 1
Ask the same question to a grounded tool AND a general chat tool
→
STEP 2
Document: Where do they agree? Where do they diverge?
→
STEP 3
Divergence = verify. Any disagreement needs a human check against the source.
✅ Both Agree
Likely correct — still cite source but higher confidence
⚠️ Partial Overlap
Core facts match but details differ — verify the details
❌ They Disagree
Stop and verify manually — one or both may be wrong
💡 This takes 2 extra minutes but catches errors that would take hours to fix later
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "Here's a simple method that catches most AI research errors."] Key talking points: • The comparison method is deceptively simple but incredibly effective • It works because different AI tools fail in different ways — they have different training data, different search strategies • When both agree, you can have higher confidence (though not certainty) • When they disagree, that's your SIGNAL — something needs checking • This is especially important for: regulatory info, statistics, recent events, technical specifications • The 3-colour system: green (agree = likely OK), amber (partial = check details), red (disagree = verify manually) Discussion prompt: "Where in your work would this 2-minute check save the most pain?"
🔨 PRACTICAL

Side-by-Side Test

  1. 1Choose a factual research question (e.g., "What are the current rules on…?" or "What changed in…?")
  2. 2Ask the exact same question to both a general AI chat AND a grounded tool
  3. 3Document your comparison:
      • What's the same in both answers?
      • What's different?
      • Which claims have sources attached?
  4. 4Verdict: Which answer would you trust for a report to your manager? Why?
⏱️ 8 minutes | Try a time-sensitive question — you'll see the biggest differences there
[TIMING: 8 min] [TRANSITION: "Let's try this comparison method right now."] Key talking points: • Students use two tools simultaneously — encourage them to use split-screen or two tabs • Best questions for this exercise: anything time-sensitive, regulatory, or quantitative • Suggested questions if stuck: "What are the current UK corporation tax rates?" / "What changed in employment law this year?" • They should document: agreements, disagreements, and which tool provides sources • Walk around and ask: "What differences did you find?" — share interesting ones with the class Facilitation tip: Time-sensitive questions work best because that's where general chat fails most obviously.

04
Verifying AI Sources
Citations aren't proof — they're claims that need checking
[TIMING: 0.5 min] [TRANSITION: "Even grounded tools can get it wrong. Chapter 4: verification."] Key talking points: • Even grounded tools aren't perfect — they can misread sources, pull from unreliable sites, or misattribute claims • A citation is a CLAIM, not proof. "According to source X" just means AI says it found it there • This chapter teaches you to verify: does the source exist, does it say what AI claims, is it authoritative? • This is the final skill that separates research professionals from AI copy-pasters

The Verification Checklist

1️⃣ Does the source exist? — Click the link. Is it a real page? Or does it 404 / lead nowhere?
2️⃣ Does it say what AI claims? — Read the actual source. Does it support the specific claim AI made? Or is AI paraphrasing loosely?
3️⃣ Is it current? — Check the publication date. Is this from 2024 or 2019? Information decays, especially in regulated fields.
4️⃣ Is it authoritative? — Is the source an official body, peer-reviewed, or an anonymous blog post? Source quality matters.
5️⃣ Is it relevant to MY question? — AI may cite a real source about a tangentially related topic. Does it actually answer YOUR specific question?
💡 You don't need to check every source — but ALWAYS check the ones that drive your key recommendation
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "Here's your 5-point checklist for verifying AI citations."] Key talking points: • This checklist works for any AI-provided citation — grounded or ungrounded • Check 1 (Exists): You'd be surprised how often AI cites papers/pages that literally don't exist • Check 2 (Says what claimed): AI often "soft-hallucinate" — the source is real but doesn't quite say what AI claimed • Check 3 (Current): A source from 2020 might be superseded by newer regulation or data • Check 4 (Authoritative): gov.uk > random blog. Peer-reviewed journal > news article • Check 5 (Relevant): AI may cite a real source about a similar but different situation • Practical rule: You don't check EVERY citation — but you MUST check the ones that drive your recommendations Discussion prompt: "Which of these 5 checks catches the most errors in your experience?"

05
The Trust Spectrum
Not everything needs the same level of verification
[TIMING: 0.5 min] [TRANSITION: "We can't verify everything — so where do we focus?"] Key talking points: • Verification takes time — we can't check every single AI claim • The skill is knowing WHEN verification is critical and when it's less so • This chapter gives you a framework for deciding where to invest your checking effort • Think of it as triage for trust: what's safe to accept, what MUST be verified?

The Trust Spectrum

✅ Safer to Trust
• Consensus facts — widely agreed, stable over time
• Well-cited claims — source provided and easily checkable
• Multiple sources agree — corroborated across tools
• General concepts — how things work in principle
• Historical facts — established, unlikely to change
🚨 Must Verify
• Recent events — anything from the last 6 months
• Niche topics — less training data = more hallucination
• Quantitative claims — statistics, percentages, figures
• Legal/medical/financial specifics — high-consequence if wrong
• Academic citations — AI's #1 hallucination category
🎯 Rule of thumb: the more specific and consequential the claim, the more you must verify
[TIMING: 4 min] [TRANSITION: "Not every AI answer needs the same level of scrutiny."] Key talking points: • Left side (safer): These are things where AI is drawing on well-established consensus — lower risk • Right side (must verify): These are AI's danger zones — recent, specific, quantitative, high-consequence • The key principle: specificity and consequence. "AI exists" needs no verification. "The fine for X is £17.5M" absolutely does • Academic citations deserve special mention — this is the #1 category where AI invents things • Practical application: spend your verification time on the RIGHT side claims, not checking everything • This isn't about laziness — it's about intelligent triage of your attention Discussion prompt: "What's on the 'must verify' side in YOUR specific role?"
